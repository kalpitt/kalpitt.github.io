Glass: an AI analyst that shows its sources

Every claim has a citation, and every number traces back to the page it came from.

Glass reads a listed Indian company's annual report and lists the accounting and solvency red flags an analyst should look into, ranked, with a severity, a confidence level and the exact source for each. It suggests what to escalate. A person decides.

Being built Mac app (Tauri) 534 automated tests Scored against hand-checked answers

The problem: nobody can check the reasoning

In June 2026, SEBI passed an ex-parte order against Rajesh Exports, alleging roughly ₹15.15 lakh crore of misrepresented revenue across FY21–25. The numbers had been public for years. It came to light through a shareholder complaint, not the audit. The information was there. Nobody had laid out the reasoning in a way others could check.

"Chat with your PDF" tools make this worse. They give confident answers that nobody can defend to an auditor or a risk committee, and in finance an answer you can't defend is no use. Glass is built so every answer can be checked.

The design: maths in code, words from the model

The most important decision is a strict split of jobs:

  • Code does all the maths. Altman Z-Score, Beneish M-Score, days sales outstanding, interest coverage and every other ratio come from tested TypeScript, checked against Enron's known numbers. The model never does arithmetic, so it can't make a number up.
  • The model (Gemini) only reads and explains. It reads the written sections, such as related-party notes, contingent liabilities and the auditor's report, and explains the computed numbers, including innocent explanations an analyst should rule out.
  • A grounding check guards the line. Every number in the model's answer must match a computed value or a figure on a cited page. Anything that doesn't is thrown away and never shown.

Flags come from a fixed list of five families: solvency, earnings quality, related-party exposure, audit signals and leverage. Each flag carries six things: the claim, the exact citation, the computed metric and its formula, the reasoning, a severity with a confidence level, and a suggested next step.

What it does today

  • Side-by-side view: click any conclusion and the source line lights up in the original PDF.
  • Ranked flag cards with severity and confidence, plus an evidence pack an analyst can export and hand to a committee.
  • Two worked examples run end to end: Rajesh Exports FY24 as the main case, and TCS FY24 as the clean comparison.
  • Your own report: Glass pulls draft figures from a PDF on your computer, you confirm each one against its source, and only confirmed figures are used. On a new report it runs the statement figures and six ratio checks; the full flag set exists only for the two worked examples so far.
  • Runs on your computer as a Mac app or in the browser. No accounts and no server of its own; the only network traffic is the calls to the model. Not released publicly yet.

How it's checked, and its limits

  • 534 automated tests (November 2026) cover the maths, the model's answers, figure extraction, the screens and the scoring itself.
  • Five sets of hand-checked answers are used to score the maths, how many real flags it finds, how many false ones it raises, and whether citations match their source.
  • Not general yet. It runs fully on two prepared reports. The work now is making it read any annual report and scoring that the same way.
  • It suggests, it never judges. Glass says what to investigate. It never says fraud.

Why I'm building it

Banks and auditors want AI they can audit. Glass is a small, working version of that: it knows what the model must never be trusted with, and a check enforces it.
